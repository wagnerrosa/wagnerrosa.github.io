<Brief>

I started from my own riding and from conversations with other riders about how they keep track of their bikes: spreadsheets, notes, apps made for cars.

Two details shaped the rest. At the pump, a rider has a helmet in one hand and a phone in the other, so logging has to cost almost nothing. And consumption is an average, so one wrong entry quietly spoils every number after it. The fastest way to log is to accept whatever gets typed or scanned, and that is exactly what an honest average can't afford.

</Brief>

<Thesis lines={["Fast in.", "Honest out."]}>Three decisions follow from it: one about effort, two about trust.</Thesis>

<Beat decision n="1" label="Effort" title={["Three steps.", "One number."]}>

The Summary, the screen a rider sees every time the app opens, took three builds. The first was a native settings-style list, complete and correct, with average km/l as one grey row among twelve. The second borrowed from Apple Health: a hero number, plus a status banner, a full-width Fuel button, coloured tiles and a Wallet-style card.

My note on it was short: simpler before, heavy now, too many buttons going to the same place. I had added instead of organising. The third build kept the hero number and the grid, moved the action to a + in the toolbar, dropped the banner (silence already means all is well) and took the colour out of the tiles.

That became the test for every screen: does this add weight or take it away? Logging got the same treatment. It is three steps, the phone fills in the date, time and place, and both numbers can be typed or read from a photo.

Even the chart waits. The first km/l needs two full tanks, so until then the Summary counts down the tanks still missing instead of drawing an empty graph.

<Beat decision n="2" label="Trust" title={["OCR suggests.", "The rider decides."]}>

Apple's Vision framework reads the photo on the phone and fills in the fields. Its mistakes look like real numbers. On a Yamaha Neo's drum odometer it lost the first digit in the bezel's shadow and stuck the tenths roller on the end: 27,119 km became 71,190, once in three tries. At a pump, R$ 28.49 became 2,849, because the decimal point on the segment display never registered.

So the design assumes the reading will sometimes be wrong. OCR only fills fields, review is mandatory, and each check turns raw input into a figure riders have a feel for: kilometres since the last fill-up, price per litre, this tank's km/l. A misread total is easy to miss. R$ 633 a litre isn't.

<Beat decision n="3" label="Honesty" title={["A gap breaks", "the measurement."]}>

Consumption is the distance between two full tanks divided by every litre added in between. Dividing each entry by the one before is simpler, and wrong as soon as someone tops up halfway. But the method assumes nothing is missing.

Forget to log three fill-ups and the kilometres stay true while the litres don't: 1,066 km over 4 litres is 266 km/l, straight into the average, the chart, the records and the medals. A ceiling of 80 km/l now drops stretches like that. The harder case is a single missed fill-up, which roughly doubles the result. Wrong, and believable.

<GapPlate caption="A worked example from the gap rule's spec, not a measurement." />

A big odometer jump has two causes that need opposite responses. A misread is a wrong number to fix. Missed fill-ups leave the number right, but the stretch before it can't count. The app can't tell which, so it asks: the odometer's check mark turns into an orange “check the km” with a button, “I filled up without logging”. Review warns at twice the bike's median km/l, about what one missed fill-up does.

A marked gap makes the next full tank a fresh start. Distance, spend and the odometer keep counting; only consumption skips the stretch, and the Summary says how many full tanks it needs to show a number again. The app never blocks a save or marks a gap by itself, since a wrong guess either way hides a real error. **I chose a missing number over a plausible lie.**

</Beat>

<Beat label="The product" title={["The same rules,", "everywhere else."]}>

The accent colour follows the active bike's manufacturer, across ten brands: the rider's identity is the motorcycle, not my logo. But Harley and KTM orange is also the colour of “service due soon”, so state colour never comes from the theme. Maintenance bars are grey on track, orange from 80% of the interval, red when overdue.

Habit matters because gaps are the problem from decision 3. There are 25 achievements and a ten-level profile on the store; levels unlock nothing, and backdated entries never earn a medal. With Premium, an earned medal can become the app icon. Group challenges, adapted from GymRats, are in TestFlight with no usage data yet.

**Measure the steps as well as the result.** I launched with fill-up events and a dashboard waiting for the third one, and nothing for the steps before it. When riders stopped, I could see where but not why. Bike-form events came in 1.1; fill-up drop-off is in 1.2. Next time, the OCR test photos come before the parser, too.

**Check what the nudges depend on.** The app asked for notification permission only after the first saved fill-up, so the riders who most needed a reminder could never get one. 1.2 asks right after the bike is registered. Shipping it is the next step, and the next thing to measure.
